About

About Verticordia Atlas

Interactive game maps that work offline, keep your progress on your own device, and do not hold the basics hostage behind a paywall. It is a passion project, and this page says plainly what that means.

A passion project by Verticordia

Why this exists

Finishing a game properly means keeping track of hundreds of collectibles, and the interactive maps that help with that tend to cap how much you can mark unless you subscribe, and to need a connection at exactly the moments you do not have one, such as on a trip. Atlas began as a small answer to that: the whole map, progress without a limit, and nothing locked away that a printed page and a pencil would have given you for free.

It was first made for me, my girlfriend, my family and a few close friends, who wanted to see games through to the end. That is why the first maps are the ones they are: the newer Resident Evil titles, Elden Ring, and The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom.

What it does

Atlas shows interactive maps for video games and remembers what you have found. It is offline first: once a map has been opened on a device, it keeps working without a connection. You can keep separate profiles for each save or playthrough, with no limit and nothing to pay, and your progress is always yours to take away, since you can export it as a JSON file whenever you like and import it back.

Beyond marking what you have found, you can add markers and categories of your own for notes and routes, select an area, even by freehand, and act on everything inside it at once, and send someone a link to a particular place on a map. There are smaller comforts too: a refined search for markers, a tidy interface, and drawing on the map as a cherry on top. An account is optional, and only needed if you want your progress to follow you between devices.

This page is not documentation, and does not explain how to use each feature. It only tells you what is there.

How it works

Each game has a single page, and much of it is generated on the fly. Your browser saves the app and the maps on your device (a service worker does the caching), and your progress is written to a database in your browser first. It only reaches our server if you sign in and ask for sync. Signing in is optional: you can start from the sign-in page, and it works in one of two ways, explained in the Terms. What we keep, and for how long, is in the Privacy Policy.

The theme toggle in the top bar switches between light and dark with a single press. Right-click it, or press and hold on a touch screen, to hand the choice back to your device (automatic).

Atlas is built on a handful of widely used open-source libraries and fonts, each credited with its licence in the Terms. The home page lists the games and maps that are available.

Our promises

No ads. No tracking. No paywalled basics.

We do not run advertising, analytics or any tracking (details in the Privacy Policy). Tracking strikes us as inelegant, and not in the spirit in which a tool like this should be made.

Everything Atlas does today will stay free. Since it runs in your browser and keeps your data on your device, there is nothing to hold back in the first place. The Terms keep the door open to paid features, and if hosting costs ever made them necessary, they would be optional extras that need our servers, never a lock on what you already have, and we would tell you first.

About the maps

The maps are fan-made reference material, in the sense the Terms describe. They grew out of the example of existing interactive map sites, which Atlas aims to match in usefulness while being more capable offline. We are rewriting and redrawing the material in our own words and hands, and if you own something shown here and want it changed or removed, tell us and we will look at it promptly.

The name

Verticordia is Latin for “turner of hearts”. It began as an old title of the Roman Venus, invoked to turn hearts from lust toward chastity. I came across it long before I was a believer, as a teenager reading Roman history and trying to teach myself Latin, and I kept it only because I liked the sound.

Years later, coming back to the Church, I understood why it had stayed with me. Purity is not pagan piety, but a fundamental virtue that nobody is saved without, and the title that the Romans originally gave to a goddess seemed to me, in the end, a fitting one for Our Lady, whom the Litany of Loreto calls Mother most pure, and Mother most chaste. Appropriating what is good in the pagan world is an old Christian habit, from St. Justin Martyr’s recognition of the seeds of the Word in the philosophers, to St. Augustine’s praise of Roman civic virtue, to St. Thomas’s synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy. Even St. Boniface, when he felled the idol-tree, took the timber and built a church with it.

Who is behind it

Verticordia is the name under which I publish my passion projects. It is a “we” on paper and one person in practice, which may change. I am a Roman Catholic from Brazil and an undergraduate in computer engineering. I was baptised as a baby, lost the faith as a teenager to bad philosophy, and found my way back through St. Thomas Aquinas, devotion to the Holy Rosary and the Traditional Latin Mass.

I was providentially exposed to the Latin Mass thanks in no small part to the evangelizing work of the Personal Apostolic Administration of Saint John Mary Vianney, its priests, and its bishop, Dom Fernando Arêas Rifan, who confirmed me, to all of whom I am forever grateful. I have not missed a Sunday Mass since.

I am a fervent Catholic, and I hope to be more fervent and more devoted each day. The work I do with technology should be coherent with that, and that is the philosophy behind anything I build, however I build it.

Almost all of this code was originally written by LLMs: Claude, Gemini and ChatGPT, working through coding agents such as OpenCode and Antigravity. I do my own editing in NeoVim, entirely in the terminal, but I wrote almost none of it by hand. I do not count that against it. I wanted a tool that works, and I do not think of code as creative work unless it is elegant for its own sake, which is not what this project is aiming at. The care went into deciding what it should do and how it should treat you.

Support and warranty

This is a spare-time project, kept up on a best-effort basis.
It comes with no warranty of any kind, as the Terms set out.

Bugs get fixed when time allows, and we cannot promise a reply to every report. The exception is anything that puts your data at risk, such as a security vulnerability. Please write to us about those at once, and we will treat them seriously.

Atlas is not an open-source project, and we are not accepting outside contributions or pull requests.
